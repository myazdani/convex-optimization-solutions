# Exercise 2.37: Nonnegative polynomials, Hankel matrices, and moments

Source: Boyd and Vandenberghe, [Convex Optimization, exercise 2.37, p. 65](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf#page=79).

Original solution.

Write $`v(t)=(1,t,\ldots,t^k)^T`$ and interpret degree $`2k`$ as degree at most $`2k`$, as the coefficient-space definition requires.

## (a) Properness of the polynomial cone

For each fixed real $`t`$, nonnegativity of the polynomial is a homogeneous closed halfspace condition on its coefficients. Their intersection is a closed convex cone. If both a polynomial and its negative are nonnegative, it vanishes everywhere and all its coefficients are zero, proving pointedness.

For solidity, use $`p_0(t)=1+t^{2k}`$ when $`k\ge1`$. A coefficient perturbation bounded by $`\epsilon`$ in absolute value has magnitude at most
```math
\epsilon\sum_{j=0}^{2k}|t|^j
\le\epsilon(2k+1)(1+t^{2k}).
```
For $`\epsilon<1/(2k+1)`$, the perturbed polynomial stays positive everywhere. This gives an open coefficient neighborhood in the cone. For $`k=0`$, the cone is simply $`\mathbb R_+`$ and is proper.

## (b) Positive semidefinite Gram representation

The stated sum-of-two-squares theorem gives coefficient vectors $`r,s\in\mathbb R^{k+1}`$ such that
```math
p(t)=(r^Tv(t))^2+(s^Tv(t))^2
=v(t)^T(rr^T+ss^T)v(t).
```
Thus $`Y=rr^T+ss^T\succeq0`$ is a Gram matrix. Expanding $`v(t)^TYv(t)`$ shows that the coefficient of $`t^{i-1}`$ is
```math
x_i=\sum_{m+n=i+1}Y_{mn},\qquad i=1,\ldots,2k+1.
```
Conversely, a matrix $`Y\succeq0`$ with these coefficient sums gives $`p(t)=v(t)^TYv(t)\ge0`$. This proves equivalence, including zero and lower-degree polynomials.

## (c) The dual Hankel cone

Let $`L(Y)`$ be the coefficient-sum map. Its adjoint is the Hankel matrix
```math
H(z)_{mn}=z_{m+n-1},\qquad 1\le m,n\le k+1,
```
because $`z^TL(Y)=\mathrm{tr}(H(z)Y)`$. Therefore
```math
z\in K_{\mathrm{pol}}^*
\ \Longleftrightarrow\ 
\mathrm{tr}(H(z)Y)\ge0\quad\forall Y\succeq0
\ \Longleftrightarrow\ H(z)\succeq0.
```
The last equivalence is self-duality of the positive semidefinite cone, or follows by testing $`Y=ww^T`$. This proves $`K_{\mathrm{pol}}^*=K_{\mathrm{han}}`$. The indexing formula also corrects a typographical entry in the displayed source matrix: its third row, last column is $`z_{k+3}`$, not $`z_{k+4}`$.

## (d) Moment representation and its converse

A finite conic combination has the form
```math
y=\sum_{j=1}^r\lambda_j(1,t_j,\ldots,t_j^{2k}),\qquad\lambda_j\ge0.
```
Its first coordinate is $`y_1=\sum_j\lambda_j`$. If it is zero then $`y=0`$. Otherwise normalize the weights to probabilities $`\lambda_j/y_1`$ to obtain the stated random-variable representation, with finite support.

Conversely, suppose $`u`$ has finite moments through order $`2k`$. Its vector $`(1,\mathbb Eu,\ldots,\mathbb Eu^{2k})`$ belongs to the convex hull of the curve $`(1,t,\ldots,t^{2k})`$, not just its closure. Here is the finite-dimensional expectation fact that justifies this possibly delicate step. If an integrable random vector takes values in a set $`S\subseteq\mathbb R^d`$, its mean belongs to $`\mathrm{conv}\,S`$. Separation first puts the mean in the closed convex hull. If it is on a relative boundary, a supporting affine functional that is nonnegative on $`S`$ has zero expectation, so it vanishes almost surely. Restrict $`S`$ to that hyperplane and repeat in the smaller affine dimension. Eventually the mean lies in the relative interior of the closed convex hull of the reduced set. Such a point lies in the convex hull itself: choose a small simplex around it in the affine hull and approximate the vertices by convex-hull points; small perturbations preserve strict inclusion in that simplex. Carathéodory's theorem then expresses the mean as a finite convex combination of points of $`S`$. The process terminates in at most $`d`$ boundary restrictions.

Applying this fact to the integrable moment vector supplies a finite-support distribution with the same moments. Multiplication by $`y_1\ge0`$ therefore gives an element of $`K_{\mathrm{mom}}`$. Finally,
```math
x\in K_{\mathrm{mom}}^*
\ \Longleftrightarrow\ 
\sum_{i=1}^{2k+1}x_it^{i-1}\ge0\quad\forall t\in\mathbb R,
```
so $`K_{\mathrm{mom}}^*=K_{\mathrm{pol}}`$.

## (e) Closure, and an explicit nonrepresentable boundary moment vector

Taking duals in part (d) and using the bipolar theorem gives
```math
\mathrm{cl}\,K_{\mathrm{mom}}
=K_{\mathrm{mom}}^{**}=K_{\mathrm{pol}}^*=K_{\mathrm{han}}.
```
For $`k=2`$ and $`z=(1,0,0,0,1)`$, $`H(z)=\mathrm{diag}(1,0,1)\succeq0`$. But a probability distribution with these moments would have $`\mathbb Eu^2=0`$, implying $`u=0`$ almost surely and hence $`\mathbb Eu^4=0`$, contradicting the last coordinate. Thus $`z\notin K_{\mathrm{mom}}`$.

For $`R\ge1`$, put mass $`1-R^{-4}`$ at zero and mass $`1/(2R^4)`$ at each of $`R,-R`$. Its moment vector is
```math
z_R=(1,0,R^{-2},0,1)\in K_{\mathrm{mom}},
\qquad z_R\longrightarrow z\quad(R\to\infty).
```
This supplies the requested explicit sequence and explains why closure cannot be omitted.
