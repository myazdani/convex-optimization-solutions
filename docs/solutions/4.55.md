# Exercise 4.55: Increasing scalar objectives and Pareto optimality

Source: [Boyd and Vandenberghe, Convex Optimization, exercise 4.55](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf#page=220). The source exercise and all of its subparts were checked directly.

Let $`x^\star`$ minimize $`\psi(f_0(x))`$ over the unchanged feasible set. If it were not Pareto optimal, a feasible $`y`$ would satisfy $`f_0(y)\preceq_K f_0(x^\star)`$ with unequal objective values. Strict $`K`$-increase of $`\psi`$ would then give $`\psi(f_0(y))<\psi(f_0(x^\star))`$, contradicting scalar optimality. Thus every scalar optimizer is Pareto optimal; no convexity hypothesis is needed.

The positive weighted sum is a special case because $`\lambda\in\mathrm{int}K^*`$ pairs strictly positively with every nonzero vector in $`K`$. Indeed if $`\lambda^Tk=0`$ for a nonzero $`k\in K`$, a sufficiently small perturbation of $`\lambda`$ in direction $`-k`$ would remain in the dual cone but have negative pairing with $`k`$, a contradiction.

For the final multicriterion example, $`\max_i u_i`$ is nondecreasing but not strictly increasing in the componentwise order. Nevertheless suppose $`x^\star`$ is its unique feasible minimizer. A dominating feasible point $`y`$ would give

```math
\max_iF_i(y)\le\max_iF_i(x^\star).
```

Strict inequality contradicts optimality. Equality makes $`y`$ another scalar minimizer; and it is distinct from $`x^\star`$ because its vector objective is distinct. This contradicts uniqueness. Hence the unique minimizer is Pareto optimal. Without uniqueness the conclusion fails, for example with feasible objective values $`(1,0)`$ and $`(1,-1)`$: both minimize the maximum, but the first is dominated.

## Validation

The proof separates strict monotonicity from uniqueness, so it does not incorrectly treat the coordinatewise maximum as strictly increasing.
