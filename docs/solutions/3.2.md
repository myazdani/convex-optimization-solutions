# Exercise 3.2: What the two contour diagrams permit

Source: Boyd and Vandenberghe, [Convex Optimization, exercise 3.2, p. 113](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf#page=127).

Original solution.

These conclusions concern what is compatible with the drawn contours; finitely many contours alone do not certify the behavior of an otherwise arbitrary function between them. The actual diagrams in the source PDF were inspected.

## First diagram

The function could be **quasiconvex**: the low-value regions are the nested convex interiors. Convex intermediate sublevel sets can be interpolated between such nested contours.

It cannot be **quasiconcave**, and therefore cannot be concave. For example, points on opposite sides of the outer contour have value three, while a suitable segment between them passes through the interior of the level-one contour. Thus a superlevel set contains both endpoints but omits an interior point of their segment.

The spacing also rules out **convexity**. Along a horizontal line through the right-hand portions of the three contours, let the successive crossing coordinates be $`s_1<s_2<s_3`$, with values $`1,2,3`$. The picture has $`s_3-s_2>s_2-s_1`$. The secant slopes are therefore
```math
\frac{2-1}{s_2-s_1}>\frac{3-2}{s_3-s_2},
```
contradicting nondecreasing secant slopes of a convex function. Equivalently the level-two convex body fails to contain the halfway Minkowski average of the level-one and level-three bodies.

## Second diagram

The high-value side is to the right of each contour. These right-hand regions are convex, so **quasiconcavity is compatible** with the drawing. The increasing spacing of successive contours in the direction of increasing values is also compatible with **concavity**, whose secant slopes decrease; the contours can be viewed as sections of nested superlevel sets of a concave surface.

The low-value side is to the left of a curved contour and is not convex: take two points on a strictly curved portion of one contour; their chord lies to its right, where values are larger. Thus the corresponding sublevel set contains the endpoints and misses chord points. Consequently the function cannot be **quasiconvex**, and hence cannot be convex.

In short: the first figure permits quasiconvexity alone among the four listed properties; the second permits concavity and quasiconcavity.
