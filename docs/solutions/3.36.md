# Exercise 3.36: Six conjugate calculations

Source: Boyd and Vandenberghe, [Convex Optimization, exercise 3.36, p. 120](https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/bv_cvxbook.pdf#page=134).

Original solution.

Throughout, $`f^*(y)=\sup_x(y^Tx-f(x))`$ over the stated domain.

## (a) Maximum coordinate

The answer is the indicator of the probability simplex: $`f^*(y)=0`$ if $`y\ge0,\mathbf1^Ty=1`$, and $`+\infty`$ otherwise. For simplex $`y`$, $`y^Tx\le\max_i x_i`$, and $`x=0`$ gives equality. If the coordinate sum differs from one, take $`x=t\mathbf1`$ with the appropriate sign and $`|t|\to\infty`$. If the sum is one but some $`y_i<0`$, set $`x_i=-t`$ and the other coordinates zero to obtain divergence.

## (b) Sum of largest coordinates

Let $`C=\{y:0\le y_i\le1,\mathbf1^Ty=r\}`$. The function is the support function of $`C`$: allocating unit capacity to the $`r`$ largest coordinates maximizes $`y^Tx`$. Thus $`f^*`$ is zero on $`C`$, since the support inequality holds and $`x=0`$ attains zero. Outside the closed convex set $`C`$, separation gives an $`x`$ with $`y^Tx>\sup_{z\in C}z^Tx`$; scaling $`x`$ proves $`f^*(y)=+\infty`$. This includes $`r=n`$ and the optional $`r=0`$ convention.

## (c) Maximum of finitely many affine functions on the line

First merge equal slopes, keeping the largest intercept; discard any genuinely redundant pieces. Let the remaining distinct slopes be $`a_1<\cdots<a_m`$. For $`m=1`$, the conjugate is $`-b_1`$ at $`y=a_1`$ and infinite elsewhere. Otherwise set
```math
\xi_i=\frac{b_i-b_{i+1}}{a_{i+1}-a_i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,m-1.
```
These are the nondecreasing successive breakpoints, with coincident breakpoints allowed for a piece active only at a point. Then
```math
f^*(y)=
\begin{cases}
(y-a_i)\xi_i-b_i,&a_i\le y\le a_{i+1},\\
+\infty,&y<a_1\text{ or }y>a_m.
\end{cases}
```
For $`y\in[a_i,a_{i+1}]`$, the slope $`y`$ is a convex combination of the two active affine slopes at $`\xi_i`$. Their same combination supplies a global affine minorant of $`f`$ touching there, proving that $`x=\xi_i`$ maximizes the conjugate expression and giving the formula. At an endpoint slope it gives $`-b_i`$ and agrees across adjacent pieces. Outside the slope range, send $`x`$ to the appropriate infinity. Equivalently, without any preprocessing,
```math
f^*(y)=\min\left\{-\sum_i\lambda_i b_i:
\lambda\ge0,\ \sum_i\lambda_i=1,\ \sum_i\lambda_i a_i=y\right\},
```
with value infinite if the constraints are infeasible; geometrically this is the lower convex boundary of the points $`(a_i,-b_i)`$.

## (d) Powers on the positive halfline

For $`p>1`$,
```math
f^*(y)=\begin{cases}
(p-1)(y/p)^{p/(p-1)},&y>0,\\
0,&y\le0.
\end{cases}
```
For $`y>0`$, the strictly concave maximand $`yx-x^p`$ has its maximum at $`x=(y/p)^{1/(p-1)}`$. For $`y\le0`$, it is negative and approaches zero as $`x\downarrow0`$.

For $`p<0`$,
```math
f^*(y)=\begin{cases}
(p-1)(y/p)^{p/(p-1)},&y<0,\\
0,&y=0,\\
+\infty,&y>0.
\end{cases}
```
The same stationary point gives the maximum for $`y<0`$, since the maximand tends to $`-\infty`$ at both endpoints. For $`y=0`$ the supremum zero is approached as $`x\to\infty`$; for $`y>0`$ the linear term diverges.

## (e) Negative geometric mean

The conjugate is
```math
f^*(y)=\begin{cases}
0,&y_i<0\ \forall i,\quad\prod_i(-y_i)\ge n^{-n},\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
```
Put $`a=-y`$. The arithmetic–geometric mean inequality gives
$`\sum_i a_ix_i\ge n(\prod_i a_i)^{1/n}(\prod_i x_i)^{1/n}`$ for positive $`a,x`$. Thus the displayed condition makes the conjugate maximand nonpositive; scaling $`x\downarrow0`$ gives supremum zero. If $`a>0`$ but the product condition fails, choose $`x_i=1/a_i`$ to make the maximand positive, then scale to infinity. A negative $`a_i`$ also immediately permits a positive maximand by increasing that coordinate. If all $`a_i\ge0`$ but one is zero, hold the positive-cost coordinates fixed and send a zero-cost coordinate to infinity, making the geometric mean unbounded while cost stays fixed. Thus all remaining cases have infinite conjugate.

## (f) Negative logarithm on the second-order cone

Use dual coordinates $`(y,s)`$ for $`(x,t)`$ and set $`\delta=s^2-\|y\|_2^2`$. The answer is
```math
f^*(y,s)=\begin{cases}
\log4-2-\log\delta,&s<-\|y\|_2,\\
+\infty,&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
```
In the finite case, stationarity of $`y^Tx+st+\log(t^2-\|x\|^2)`$ gives
```math
x=\frac{2y}{\delta},\qquad t=-\frac{2s}{\delta},\qquad
t^2-\|x\|^2=\frac4\delta.
```
This point is in the cone domain; convexity of the original barrier makes stationarity sufficient for the global conjugate maximum. Substitution gives the stated value.

If $`s>-\|y\|`$, some strict-cone direction has positive linear pairing, and scaling it makes the objective diverge. At $`(y,s)=0`$, scaling any direction makes the logarithm diverge. On the remaining boundary, write $`a=\|y\|>0`$, $`s=-a`$, and take a direction $`x=(1-\epsilon)y/a,t=1`$. Its pairing is $`-a\epsilon`$ and its cone quadratic is $`2\epsilon-\epsilon^2`$. Scaling by $`2/(a\epsilon)`$ yields value
$`-2+\log(4(2\epsilon-\epsilon^2)/(a^2\epsilon^2))\to+\infty`$. This proves the strict dual-domain condition.
